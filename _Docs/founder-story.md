## Why I built this

I was a child who could sing. My teachers knew it; I knew it. But when the
moment came to stand on a stage and let anyone hear me, I couldn't. No one had
taught me it was safe to be seen — to be imperfect in front of other people —
so I stayed in my seat. I never performed. The voice I had simply faded. I'm an
adult now, and I'm still afraid of stages. That small, private regret is one of
the reasons this centre exists.

I grew up inside the Chinese education system, in Jiangsu, where the path to a
future runs across what we call the *du mu qiao* — the single-plank bridge that
thousands cross and few get over. I made it; I finished in the top 5%. I don't
resent the discipline that took. It gave me foundations, and a belief I still
hold: that ability is *built* through effort, not handed out at birth.

But when I ask myself what learning actually stayed with me, it isn't the exams.
It's my grandparents' village. The river where we caught crabs and fish. Stones
and rubber bands for toys. Drawing on the floor. Turning a corner of the yard
into a house, a police station, a whole world. We learned to read and write from
the older children long before any teacher made it formal. That was real
learning too — maybe the realest. My own memory had been telling me all along:
play is not the opposite of learning.

My husband grew up in Singapore, where discipline sits at the centre of
childhood. Between us we carried two versions of structure, and a real respect
for what it gives a child — focus, self-control, the habits that make everything
else possible.

So when it was our turn — our daughter, Alexandra — we couldn't stop asking the
question. We read, we argued, we tried childcare and kindergarten in China, in
Singapore, and here in Australia. And in Australia I found the thing my own
childhood had been missing a name for: children trusted to follow their own
curiosity, playing outdoors in real weather, allowed to be confident and to
speak — to wonder out loud, to take up space.

I don't want to choose between these worlds. I want the best of all three, for
Alexandra and for every child here: the foundations and the belief in effort I
learned in China; the structure and focus my husband learned in Singapore; the
curiosity, nature, and confidence I found in Australia. Not one at the cost of
another — each in its right place. Structure to give a child security. Freedom
to let them explore. And, above all, the thing I was never given — the
encouragement to be brave. To try. To be wrong out loud. To take the stage.

Because a child can be capable and still be afraid to be seen. I was. I don't
want that for my daughter, and I don't want it for yours. I want them to leave
us with real foundations *and* the confidence to use them — ready for school,
and ready for a life that will ask them, again and again, to step up and be
heard.

That's the centre I went three countries to learn how to build. It carries my
daughter's name. I'd love to show it to you.

## Editorial notes (decisions I made — all reversible)

- **It opens on the stage, not the bridge.** The singing regret is your most
  distinctive and human moment, and it sets up the whole "courage to be seen"
  thesis. If opening on a private regret feels too exposed for a public page, we
  swap to the **village** as the opener (warmer, more universal) and bring the
  stage in later. Your call.
- **"Top 5%" is in, lightly.** It's earned and credible to your Asian audience,
  who read the *du mu qiao* instantly. To some Australian readers, a stated rank
  can sound boastful. Option: keep it, or soften to "I made it across." Flag
  which you prefer.
- **I deliberately did *not* contrast against Asian reserve.** Notice the
  Australia paragraph frames confidence as something I *found and wanted*, never
  as "unlike reserved Asian children." That protects you with the structure-
  valuing families you're courting — honour, then add.
- **The close hands off to the tour**, not to more about you. The story is the
  vehicle; the tour is the destination.
